## Section 1: Communications Research
**LO 4.1**

### Core Content

The IMC planning process begins with research [Textbook, IM, PPT]. Three approaches, in order:

1. **Product-specific research**: identifies the product characteristics that become selling points. Goes beyond attributes to benefits. Example given: a mobile-device marketing team learns some apps get used to pass time while others deliver functional information (like restaurant location), and one of those becomes the focal point of the message [Textbook].
2. **Consumer-oriented research**: understands the *context* of product use through three lenses [Textbook, PPT]:
   - **Anthropological**: direct observation of consumers using the product at home, at work, on vacation.
   - **Sociological**: social class, trends, family life-cycle changes.
   - **Psychological**: motives like feeling sexy, powerful, or intelligent.
3. **Target-market research**: identifies who should actually receive the campaign, by combining what product research and consumer research revealed [Textbook].

Agencies often supplement all three with a **focus group**: consumers talking about a topic, product, or brand in front of a moderator who tracks comments and ideas [Textbook, IM, Test Bank Q22].

### Talk-Through Examples

1. **Scotts Miracle-Gro and "Scotts" (consumer-oriented, psychological/sociological).** [Textbook] Before building a fertilizer campaign, The Richards Group interviewed 4,000 consumers and found people would change lawn-care behavior if an expert told them to. Many said they personally knew a neighborhood "lawn expert." That insight produced the spokesperson "Scotts" (a mnemonic play on the brand name), and the campaign tested well with consumers.
2. **"Got milk?" (product-specific + consumer-oriented).** [Test Bank Q3] The famous campaign emerged after consumers were deprived of milk for a week; the correct research pairing per the test bank is product-specific and consumer-oriented research, not target-market research. This is a good one to walk through live since it is also a common trick question (students often want to add target-market research).
3. **S'well water bottles (all three types, in sequence).** [Textbook Critical Thinking 4-21] Walk through this one as a mini case: as a marketing intern for S'well, which research type tells you what to say about the product (double-wall insulation, no condensation), which tells you why someone buys it (status, sustainability, hydration psychology), and which tells you who to target (frequent travelers, office workers, students)?

### Instructor Notes

- [PPT speaker notes] Emphasize to students that "consumers buy benefits, not attributes," so even product-specific research should end in a benefit statement, not just a features list.
- [Test Bank] Item 5 and several others test that **statistics/data analysis is not one of the three named research approaches** (the three are product-specific, consumer-oriented, target-market). This is a reliable trap answer across multiple test bank items (Q2, Q5, Q14) worth flagging explicitly.
- [IM] The IM's version of this section is nearly identical in structure to the textbook but strips out the Scotts Miracle-Gro/Richards Group example and the S'well/Elephant Pants brand names, using only generic language ("product-specific research involves identifying key product characteristics..."). If you are working from the IM alone, you will want to borrow the textbook's brand examples to keep the lecture narratable.

### Online-Adapted Activity

**Discussion board, no synchronous component:** Assign each student one of five real products (a meal-kit service, a rideshare app, a national gym chain, a skincare brand, a pet-insurance company). In a short discussion post (150 words), they identify one research question for each of the three research types, then reply to a classmate assigned a different product, comparing how the *type* of research question changes even though the three categories stay the same.

## Section 2: Consumer Market Segmentation I — Demographics, Psychographics & VALS
**LO 4.2**

### Core Content

A market segment is "a set of businesses or a group of individual consumers with distinct characteristics" [Textbook, Test Bank Q27]. To be **viable**, a segment must be [Textbook, PPT, IM, Test Bank Q29, Q78]:

- Homogeneous internally
- Distinct from the general population and from other segments
- Large enough to be financially worth a separate campaign
- Reachable through some media or communications channel

**Demographics** (population characteristics: gender, age, education, income, ethnicity) is the most common and easiest segmentation approach [Textbook, PPT].

- **Gender**: Not all individuals conform to one gender role; Gen Z and many millennial parents want gender-inclusive brand experiences and gender-neutral toys/clothing [Textbook]. Men shop more than they used to, focus on product performance over browsing, and favor recognizable brand names that signal status [Textbook]. Women control roughly two-thirds of global consumer spending, are primary household shoppers about 75% of the time, yet 91% say advertisers do not understand them [Textbook, PPT].
- **Age**: Combine with other variables for precision (older women + osteoporosis products; young working mothers + convenience foods) [Textbook]. Children form brand-logo mental images by 6 months old and recognize up to 100 brand images by age 3 [Textbook]. Tweens: 56% want instant gratification, 59% want customized products, 55% influence household movie choices (Figure 4.3) [Textbook, PPT].
- **Age and Lifestage**: age alone is a blunt instrument; a young adult (18-30) could be single, newly married, or a new parent, and that lifestage detail sharpens targeting far more than age alone [Textbook, PPT].
- **Income**: lower income households buy necessities; higher income households buy sundries and eventually luxuries. The "exhausted affluent" ($100,000 to $150,000 household income) identify more with the working class than "the haves," and want style and function without ostentation [Textbook].
- **Ethnicity**: Minorities represent $2.5 trillion in buying power; 70% of ethnic minorities say ethnicity is a significant part of personal identity [Textbook]. The trend has shifted from separate ethnic campaigns to a single holistic campaign incorporating multiple ethnic insights [Textbook, IM, Test Bank Q69-70].
- **Psychographics** (AIO: activities, interests, opinions) explain the *why* behind demographics [Textbook, PPT]. The **VALS typology** (Strategic Business Insights) sorts consumers into 8 groups: Innovators, Thinkers, Achievers, Experiencers, Believers, Strivers, Makers, Survivors [Textbook, PPT, IM, Test Bank Q43-45].

### Talk-Through Examples

1. **Degree and Old Spice (gender, moving toward gender-neutral positioning).** [Textbook] Degree launched unisex in 1990, later split into Degree Men/Women with different appeals (marks-on-clothing / social durability for women, mountain biking and snowboarding for men), then reunited both lines under one campaign and tagline, "Do More," featuring athletes training outside their primary sport. Old Spice ran a similar 2019 "Lavender" campaign with a male and female character fighting over who gets to use the product.
2. **Chrysler Ram trucks, Kraft Mac & Cheese, and Domino's (ethnicity, holistic approach).** [Textbook] Chrysler built a Hispanic-market campaign around the Ram 1500/2500 using unscripted, real-life testimonials, themed "A Todo Con Todo" ("To everything, with everything"), rather than simply translating an English-language ad. Kraft's "Mantente 100%" campaign told Hispanic consumers that eating Mac & Cheese does not make them "less Hispanic." Domino's built ads around Mauricio Arroyave, a Colombian immigrant who owns ten franchise locations. This is a strong three-brand comparison for showing what "holistic, not translated" ethnic marketing looks like in practice.
3. **Home Depot as Makers, Siri as Innovators (VALS applied).** [Textbook] The chapter directly ties VALS segments to real brand fit: Home Depot's core shopper looks like a "Maker" (self-sufficient, hands-on, unimpressed by status symbols), while an early adopter product like Siri would have first needed to reach "Innovators" (sophisticated, tech-receptive, upscale tastes).

### Instructor Notes

- [Test Bank] Multiple items (Q36, Q64, Q67) test that selling to women, or to a specific ethnic group, is a **demographic**, not psychographic, decision purely by itself; students conflate the two often enough that this is worth a direct, explicit correction on screen.
- [PPT speaker notes] slide 20 gives a compact contrast worth reading aloud: "reaching achievers requires ads that stress careers, families, goals... reaching experiencers requires ads that convey youthfulness, enthusiasm, impulsiveness."
- [IM] covers demographics, psychographics, and VALS in the same order and with the same 8-segment list, but without any of the Degree/Old Spice, Chrysler/Kraft/Domino's, or Home Depot/Siri examples above. If teaching from the IM alone, the lecture will be accurate but flat; pull in the textbook's named brands for narration.

### Online-Adapted Activity

**Shared collaborative doc:** Post a shared Google Doc with the 8 VALS segments as column headers. Each student adds one row: a real brand or ad they have seen, which VALS segment it targets, and one sentence of evidence from the ad itself. Students may not duplicate a brand already listed. Close the activity with a short recorded video (2-3 minutes) walking through the three or four most interesting entries.

## Section 3: Consumer Market Segmentation II — Generations, Geographic, Geodemographic, Benefit & Usage
**LO 4.2 (continued)**

### Core Content

**Generational segmentation** assumes shared adolescent/early-adult experiences shape lifelong values, tastes, and preferences (Figure 4.4) [Textbook, PPT]:

| Segment | Birth Years | Core Trait |
|---|---|---|
| Generation Z | 1995-2010 | "Seekers of truth," grew up digital, avoid labels |
| Millennials | 1978-2002 | Spend on clothes, cars, college; ~90% live at home/dorm/rent |
| Generation X | 1965-1977 | Focus on family; spend on food, housing, transport |
| Younger Boomers | 1954-1964 | Home/family focus; mortgages, toys, recreation gear |
| Older Boomers | 1952-1953 | Home upgrades, kids' education, luxury (boats) |
| Seniors | Up to 1951 | Fixed income; heavy healthcare spending |

**Geographic segmentation (geo-targeting)** limits messages to a region; smartphone GPS has made this far more precise (Home Depot sending snow-removal ads to storm-hit regions and lawn-care ads elsewhere, same day) [Textbook].

**Geodemographic segmentation** layers demographic + geographic + psychographic data. **Claritas PRIZM** is the named example: **66 market segments** mapped to every U.S. ZIP code, on the premise that people self-sort into neighborhoods of similar people [Textbook, PPT]. *(Edition flag: the IM says 62 segments; use 66, the textbook/PPT figure, as current.)*

**Benefit segmentation** groups by the advantage sought, not by who the customer is [Textbook, PPT, Test Bank Q53]. Fitness industry example: Winners (exercise because they love it), Dieters (women 35+, weight maintenance, hospital/nutritionist wellness programs), Self-Improvers (medically directed, cost-control motivated).

**Usage segmentation** clusters by purchase behavior: heavy, average, light, nonuser [Textbook, PPT]. Often 10-30% of customers generate 70-90% of sales.

### Talk-Through Examples

1. **Millennial sub-segments and the grill brands (generations, real data).** [Textbook] A Turn Inc. study split millennials into four groups: struggling aspirationals (largest, bargain-hunting but aspirational), successful homeowners (highest income, own homes), active affluents (outdoor/travel, new parents), comfortable TV watchers (screen-centered). Because so few millennials own homes, grill brands Weber, Char-Broil, and Lynx shifted from TV to digital display, social, and mobile ads, and built companion apps for grill temperature monitoring, aimed at renters who tailgate and camp rather than homeowners with a backyard patio.
2. **Dairy Queen and Carnival Cruise Lines (usage segmentation, light users and nonusers).** [Textbook] Dairy Queen targeted once-a-month light users with "Fan food, not fast food," trying to bump them to two or three visits monthly. Carnival built twelve humorous "land versus sea" ads specifically for the 76% of Americans who had never cruised, directly countering the "too expensive, too cramped, too structured" objections.
3. **Applied Microbiology (geodemographic, budget outcome included).** [Textbook] Targeting dairy farms with 1,000+ head of cattle using geodemographics cut a planned $3 million national launch budget to $1 million while increasing sales, versus the traditional national agricultural-magazine approach. This example doubles as a strong bridge into Section 4 (B2B) and Section 7 (budgeting), since it is one of the few places in the chapter where a real before/after dollar figure appears.

### Instructor Notes

- [Test Bank] Q46-52 cluster heavily on distinguishing geographic vs. geodemographic vs. benefit segmentation using single-sentence scenarios; these make excellent quick-check quiz items since the wording is very close between answer options.
- [PPT] slide 24 names the three fitness benefit segments identically to the textbook (Winners, Dieters, Self-Improvers), so this is a safe one to build a graphic around.
- [IM] states PRIZM identifies 62 segments (older figure); correct this explicitly on screen or in the transcript if you present both sources' language, since a student comparing IM and textbook side by side will notice the discrepancy.

### Online-Adapted Activity

**Recorded video response (60-90 seconds), no live small groups:** Each student picks one segmentation type from this section (generational, geographic, geodemographic, benefit, or usage) and records themselves pitching a 15-second "ad concept" for a fictional energy drink aimed at that segment, then narrates why they made the choices they did. Post to the discussion board as a video reply; classmates comment on which segmentation type is easiest to spot from the video alone.

## Section 4: Business-to-Business Market Segmentation
**LO 4.3**

### Core Content

B2B segmentation groups similar organizations into clusters for tailored messaging and service (Figure 4.5) [Textbook, PPT]:

1. **Industry (NAICS code)**: North American Industry Classification System, 20 broad sectors, six-digit codes, replacing the older SIC system [Textbook, IM]. Example: health care and social assistance = sector 62, with subsectors 621 (Ambulatory Care), 622 (Hospitals), 623 (Nursing/Residential Care), 624 (Social Assistance) [Textbook].
2. **Size of business**: large, mid-size, and small firms warrant different contact methods (outside sales for large accounts, phone/digital-triggered outside sales for mid-size, email/inbound telemarketing for small) [Textbook].
3. **Geographic location**: works especially well where an industry clusters physically (Silicon Valley) [Textbook, PPT].
4. **Product usage**: how the business customer actually uses the good or service, since one service (financing, protection, shipping) may serve very different use cases [Textbook].
5. **Customer value**: businesses sorted into low/medium/high value tiers using sales records and other data; easier for B2B than consumer marketing due to richer per-customer data [Textbook, Test Bank Q97-98].

### Talk-Through Examples

1. **Applied Microbiology, revisited for B2B (geographic + product usage).** [Textbook] Same case as Section 3, now framed as B2B geodemographic targeting: large dairy operations (1,000+ cows) were treated as both bigger buyers and opinion leaders whose adoption would pull smaller farms along, with testimonials (including a Cornell University validation) feeding follow-on direct mail to surrounding farms.
2. **Reynolds Protection (product usage segmentation).** [Textbook, Test Bank Q96] One company, one core service (protection), segmented into three distinct use cases: hostile meetings, workplace threats, and employee terminations, each requiring its own marketing materials even though the underlying service barely changes.
3. **NAICS 62 walk-through (industry segmentation, concrete numbers).** [Textbook] Use the four-subsector health care breakdown (621/622/623/624) as a live example of how one manufacturer of health-related products could build four distinct outreach lists from a single NAICS sector, then note that Ambulatory Care (621) further breaks into physicians, dentists, chiropractors, and optometrists.

### Instructor Notes

- [Test Bank] Several items (Q83-86) are "except" format questions confirming psychographics/demographics/generations are **not** B2B segmentation variables (only industry, size, geography, usage, customer value are). Good rapid-fire quiz material.
- [IM] The IM's review question 4-12 (NAICS) states the system "broadens the product categories from 10 to 20," which is consistent with the textbook's "20 broad sectors" language, so this particular fact is *not* an edition mismatch, unlike the PRIZM figure above.
- [Textbook] Case 1 in the textbook (Landscape Designs and Moore) is explicitly built around a B2B/consumer dual-segment scenario (homeowners vs. property/mall managers) and pairs naturally with this section; see Activity Bank below.

### Online-Adapted Activity

**Discussion board with structured prompts:** Assign students a real local or well-known B2B company (a commercial landscaping firm, a payroll-services provider, an industrial cleaning supplier). In a post, they identify which of the five B2B segmentation methods that company most likely relies on and why, citing one specific piece of evidence from the company's actual website. Require at least one reply that respectfully challenges a classmate's segmentation choice with an alternative reading of the same evidence.

## Section 5: Product Positioning
**LO 4.4**

### Core Content

Product positioning is the perception in the consumer's mind of a company/brand relative to competitors, built from product quality, price, distribution, packaging, and image [Textbook, PPT, Test Bank Q107-108]. Two elements matter most: how customers evaluate the brand, and how it stands relative to competition. Seven approaches (Figure 4.6) [Textbook, PPT]:

1. **Attributes**: a trait/characteristic that differentiates.
2. **Competitors**: position by direct contrast to a named rival.
3. **Use or application**: a memorable use-case becomes the position.
4. **Price-quality relationship**: compete at either the high or low end of price/quality.
5. **Product user**: define the brand by who uses it.
6. **Product class**: position relative to a category, sometimes by creating a new one.
7. **Cultural symbol**: hardest to achieve, strongest payoff when it works.

A brand's position is never permanently fixed; competitors and market shifts can force a repositioning decision [Textbook].

### Talk-Through Examples

1. **Attributes and Competitors, side by side.** [Textbook] Dove Men+Care built its "Tough on sweat, not on skin" position around a genuine product attribute (moisturizers in the deodorant), fronted by Shaquille O'Neal. Avis, by contrast, built decades of advertising on a direct competitor contrast with Hertz, turning "we're not #1" into "we try harder." Chevrolet ran comparison ads directly against Ford and Toyota. Use these three back to back to show how differently "attributes" and "competitors" positioning read on screen even when the underlying goal (differentiation) is the same.
2. **Use/Application and Price-Quality, with a real quote.** [Textbook] Arm & Hammer has spent years teaching consumers new *uses* for baking soda (refrigerator deodorizer, toothpaste ingredient, co-branded with OxiClean for pet-odor removal); Tide Pods and dishwasher pod competitors (Cascade, Finish) used the same use/application logic to remove the guesswork of measuring detergent. On price-quality, Hallmark's tagline for those who "only want to send the very best" sits at the high end, while Walmart's "Save Money, Live Better" explicitly reframes low price as a *quality-of-life* benefit rather than just cheapness.
3. **Product Class and Cultural Symbol, with Denny's own CMO quote.** [Textbook] Denny's repositioned from "family restaurant" to "diner" after focus groups rejected the family-restaurant label; CMO Frances Allen said customers "think of the brand as a diner, with great comfort food at a great price, and they feel that incredible warmth and connection to the servers. There's a soul to a diner that is very authentic, very warm, and very accepting." Swiffer created an entirely new "Quick Clean" product class rather than competing inside "mops" or "brooms." For cultural symbol, Chevrolet's long-running "American as baseball and apple pie" positioning, most recently revived for the Silverado during the MLB All-Star Game, is the textbook's own example of a brand that successfully claimed this hardest-to-achieve position.

### Instructor Notes

- [PPT speaker notes] slide 29 makes an important nuance explicit: price-quality positioning can go two directions, "emphasizing value (low price)" or "emphasizing high quality, with little mention of the higher price." Students often assume "price-quality" positioning only means "premium," so this is worth stating out loud.
- [Test Bank] this LO has the single largest cluster of scenario-based questions in the whole test bank (roughly items 106-133), almost all phrased as "Brand X does Y, which positioning approach is this?" This section is the highest-yield one for building a timed, auto-graded quiz.
- [IM] gives the same seven categories with brief, brand-free definitions and does not include the Denny's quote, the Avis/Hertz history, or the Silverado/All-Star Game detail; all three came from the textbook narrative only.

### Online-Adapted Activity

**Embedded quiz-style check, self-paced:** Build a short auto-graded quiz (5-7 items) using real, unattributed brand descriptions drawn from outside the textbook (a locally known restaurant, a regional bank, a well-known app) and ask students to identify which of the seven positioning approaches is at work, with immediate feedback text explaining the correct answer. This replaces a live "cold call the class" positioning drill.

## Section 6: Marketing Communications Objectives
**LO 4.5**

### Core Content

Communications objectives tie context, target market, and positioning to budget and IMC component decisions, and guide creative teams in building actual messages [Textbook, PPT]. Figure 4.7 shows overall company objectives cascading into targeted communications objectives:

| Overall Company Objective | Targeted Communications Objective(s) |
|---|---|
| Market Share | Build customer traffic; encourage repeat purchases; enhance purchase actions; reassurance after purchase |
| Total Annual Sales | Increase sales, overall brand / specific products / specific campaigns |
| Profits | Target or improve marketing expenditures |
| Return on Investment | Enhance or increase brand awareness |
| Brand Loyalty | Enhance or change/shift customer beliefs and attitudes |
| Position in the Industry | Increase category demand via brand equity; enhance/improve image |

A **benchmark measure** is the baseline used to evaluate change after a campaign runs [Textbook, PPT, Test Bank Q136, Q139].

### Talk-Through Examples

1. **Bombas (a single ad serving two objectives at once).** [Textbook] Bombas ads note product quality *and* the company's practice of donating socks to people in need, in the same spot, illustrating that one execution can pursue brand awareness and image enhancement simultaneously rather than requiring separate campaigns for each objective.
2. **Working the Figure 4.7 ladder top to bottom.** Walk through a single hypothetical brand (a regional coffee chain) and have students watch you move down the chart live: starting objective is "increase market share," which cascades to "encourage repeat purchases" (a punch-card loyalty app), which in turn needs a benchmark (current average visits per customer per month) before you can say the campaign "worked."
3. **Distinguishing marketing objectives from communications objectives (a frequent test bank trap).** [Test Bank Q134-135, Q137-138] Sales volume, market share, profits, and ROI are *marketing* objectives, general and company-wide; brand awareness, category demand, and belief change are the *communications* objectives that support them. "Assessing the economic environment" and "increasing the number of products offered" are neither, and are the correct "except" answers on two separate test bank items.

### Instructor Notes

- [PPT] Note that the deck presents Figure 4.7 as a diagram without full note text, while the textbook spells out every cell; if borrowing the PPT visual, supplement narration from the textbook table above.
- [Test Bank] This is a relatively small item cluster (roughly Q134-139) compared to segmentation and positioning, so a short, focused 3-4 item check is proportionate rather than a full quiz.

### Online-Adapted Activity

**Shared collaborative doc, ladder exercise:** Post the Figure 4.7 table as a doc. Assign each student a different overall company objective (market share, sales, profits, ROI, brand loyalty, industry position) and have them add one *original* (non-textbook) real brand example illustrating how that overall objective could cascade into one of the listed targeted objectives, with a one-sentence justification.

## Section 7: Communications Budgets, Schedules & IMC Components
**LO 4.6**

### Core Content

**Six budgeting methods** (Figure 4.8) [Textbook, PPT, IM]:

1. **Percentage of sales**: simple, but moves in the wrong direction (budget shrinks exactly when sales, and often the need for advertising, are down).
2. **Meet the competition**: prevents share loss in highly competitive categories; does not guarantee efficient spending.
3. **"What we can afford"**: set only after every other budget line is finalized; signals marketing is viewed as a cost, not an investment; common among newer/smaller firms.
4. **Objective and task**: sum the actual cost of achieving each stated communications objective; widely regarded by marketing experts as the best method, though harder for a company with hundreds of products (like Procter & Gamble) to execute cleanly.
5. **Payout planning**: sets an advertising-to-sales/share ratio, front-loaded early (build awareness and equity), reduced later as the brand matures and returns diminish.
6. **Quantitative models**: computer simulations linking ad spend to sales/profit; mostly available to larger firms with strong data/statistics teams.

**Three communications schedules**: pulsating (continuous with intensity bursts), flighting (peak times only, silent otherwise), continuous (level spending year-round) [Textbook, PPT].

**IMC components**: media advertising is only about 40% of a typical marketing communications budget; trade and consumer promotions each take roughly another 25% [Textbook]. Spend as a percent of sales varies enormously by category: about 25% for transportation services versus 0.1% for office furniture [Textbook].

### Talk-Through Examples

1. **Credit card industry (meet-the-competition, real dollar figure).** [Textbook] Industry ad spend "now exceeds $2.4 trillion per year" (chapter's figure), with brand recognition tracking spend level even though market share does not move in lockstep, since recognition takes time to convert to actual card signups.
2. **Retail and fitness/diet brands (all three schedule types, contrasted live).** [Textbook] Target, Macy's, and Kohl's run **pulsating** schedules (year-round with back-to-school and Black-Friday-into-Christmas bursts). Weight Watchers, Diet Centers, and Peloton lean **flighting**, concentrated in the first weeks of January into February (New Year's resolutions) with a second wave before swimsuit season. Kenmore, Whirlpool, and GE run **continuous** schedules because dishwashers and refrigerators are bought "as needed," so staying visible at an unpredictable purchase moment matters more than bursting around a calendar event.
3. **Applied Microbiology dollar figures, revisited a third time.** [Textbook] $3 million traditional plan versus $1 million using geodemographic targeting is worth bringing back here specifically because it is one of the only places in the whole chapter where a real "here is what the budget method saved" number appears; use it to make the abstract budgeting methods concrete.

### Instructor Notes

- [Test Bank] Budgeting and scheduling together account for a large share of the test bank (roughly Q140-187), heavily scenario-based ("if Burger King matches McDonald's spending, which method is this?"). This is the second-highest-yield section for quiz building, after positioning.
- [PPT speaker notes] explicitly states objective-and-task is "now being used by about half of all firms," a specific figure worth reading aloud since students tend to remember concrete percentages better than the qualitative "many experts believe" framing.
- [IM] covers all six budget methods and three schedules with matching definitions but no dollar figures or named brands (no $2.4 trillion credit card stat, no Target/Kohl's/Peloton examples); pull those details from the textbook when lecturing from IM notes alone.

### Online-Adapted Activity

**Embedded quiz-style check plus short discussion post:** After a brief auto-graded quiz matching six budget methods to six one-line scenarios, follow with a discussion prompt: "Pick a local small business you know (a coffee shop, a gym, a salon). Which of the six budgeting methods do you think they actually use, and which one *should* they use instead? Defend your answer in under 150 words."

## Section 8: International IMC Planning
**LO 4.7**

### Core Content

Globally integrated marketing communications (GIMC) programs require [Textbook, PPT, Figure 4.9]:

- Understanding the international market (language, culture, laws)
- Creating a **borderless marketing plan**: one basic approach across markets
- **"Think globally, act locally"**: subsidiaries adapt implementation within that shared plan
- **Local partnerships**: research or ad firms fluent in local language/culture, sometimes via a **cultural assimilator** who understands both markets [IM]
- **Communication segmentation**: a package that reaches every viable target market in the new country
- **Market communications analysis**: local competitor strengths/weaknesses and open opportunities
- **Solid communications objectives**, set only after the above

### Talk-Through Examples

1. **Ruth's Chris Steak House, "we sell sizzle as well as steaks."** [Textbook] This tagline could not be translated literally into Spanish because no equivalent word for "sizzle" exists; the translator instead found a Spanish idiom carrying the same meaning. This is the chapter's cleanest single example of "borderless plan, locally adapted execution" in action, at the level of a single word choice.
2. **Cultural assimilator role.** [IM] The IM adds a detail not spelled out as explicitly in the textbook narrative: some local partnerships are formed specifically by hiring a "cultural assimilator," someone who understands the local market and speaks both the parent company's language and the local region's language. Useful for making "local partnerships" concrete rather than abstract.
3. **Contrast with the domestic segmentation work already covered.** Have students explicitly connect back: the same demographic, psychographic, and positioning tools from Sections 2, 3, and 5 still apply internationally, they simply need re-validation in each new market rather than being assumed to transfer automatically.

### Instructor Notes

- [Test Bank] This is the smallest item cluster in the whole bank (Q188-191, only 4 items), so a single quick knowledge check is proportionate; do not over-build a quiz here.
- [IM/Textbook alignment] Unlike the PRIZM figure, this section shows no meaningful edition drift between IM and textbook; both describe the same seven GIMC ingredients in the same order.

### Online-Adapted Activity

**Discussion board:** Post a real, short marketing tagline in English (can reuse "Save Money, Live Better" or another from Section 5) and ask students to identify one word or phrase that would be genuinely difficult to translate directly into another language they know (or have studied), and to propose an idiom or rework that preserves the meaning, the way the Ruth's Chris translator did with "sizzle."
